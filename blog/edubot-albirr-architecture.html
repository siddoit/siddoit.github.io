Meet Albirr, the Robot That Remembers Your Face

Published: June 18, 2026 · Robotics

Most "AI robots" are a speaker with a chatbot behind it. Albirr is different: it watches who walks in, knows the difference between you and a stranger, and says hello in your language — all on a Raspberry Pi 5, with no cloud round-trip slowing it down.

The hard part was never the talking. It was keeping a robot calm while it does three things at once: see, listen, and speak. Early versions froze the camera the moment the voice engine woke up. So the whole system was rebuilt around one rule — nothing blocks anything else.

Seeing without lag

A background thread constantly drains the camera buffer, so Albirr is always looking at the freshest frame, never a stale one from half a second ago. Faces are found with YuNet and recognized with SFace, and a Kalman filter plus the Hungarian algorithm keep track of who's who even as people move and overlap. The neat trick is identity protection: if a new person steps into a spot someone else was using, the old tracking memory is wiped instantly. No more "ghost" of the previous person haunting the new one.

Speaking without thinking (too hard)

If Albirr already knows you, it skips the language model entirely and fires a greeting straight to the speakers — "Assalamu Alaikum" — fast enough that it feels like a person noticing you. For everyone else, a tiered check runs: a local face cache first, then a quick async lookup in the cloud. Voice itself is generated to a RAM disk and played from there, which shaved the "thinking" pause down to almost nothing. Getting Malayalam to sound natural took tuning the voice's speed and pitch until the syllables stopped sounding robotic.

A robot that talks like a five-year-old

The personality lives in a single locked-down system prompt. Albirr is told to be concise, to mix in everyday Manglish ("Sheri aanu", "Nokkoo"), and to answer in one sentence. The point wasn't to show off a giant model — it was to make a tool a child would actually sit with. That constraint, more than any model choice, is what makes it feel like a tutor rather than a search box.

Why it's interesting

Albirr is a small demonstration of a bigger idea: useful robots don't need a data center, they need good plumbing. Separate the streams, protect identities, and let the cloud be a fallback instead of a crutch. Build it that way and a $50 board starts to feel alive.
