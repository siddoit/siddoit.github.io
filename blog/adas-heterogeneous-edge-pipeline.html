How a $400 Phone Learned to Drive

Published: July 10, 2026 · Edge AI

Everyone says you need a $2,000 compute box to make a car drive itself. I wanted to find out what happens if you hand the job to the phone already in your pocket — a Galaxy S23, with a chip designed for TikTok, not Teslas.

The first attempt was humbling. I lined up the models the way you would on a laptop: run them one after another and hope for the best. On a phone, that hope evaporates fast. The chip got hot, the system bus clogged, and the whole rover eventually just gave up. The lesson was obvious — a Snapdragon isn't one computer, it's three, and treating it like one is why edge AI projects die.

Three brains, one car

The Snapdragon 8 Gen 2 has a GPU, an NPU, and a CPU, each good at very different things. So I stopped asking one of them to do everything and gave each a role it's built for:

  • The GPU draws the road — dense lane segmentation with Fast-SCNN, computing the curves the car should follow.
  • The NPU spots the things — high-speed object detection with YOLO and RT-DETR.
  • The CPU keeps time — syncing the camera and crunching the steering math dozens of times a second.

Split that way, the same workloads that crashed the rover now run cool and steady. It's less "smarter model" and more "stop making the wrong silicon do the wrong job."

The transformer that wouldn't compile

Detection transformers like RT-DETR are elegant on a server and hostile on a phone. Their shapes shift mid-flight, and mobile compilers simply refuse to process them. The fix was unglamorous: I wrote a tracing script that froze the dimensions, pinned the attention queries, and flattened the model down to operators the hardware actually understands. Vision transformers aren't impossible on edge — they're just demanding to be translated first.

Letting the compiler do the yelling

Getting models onto the phone meant handing them to Qualcomm's AI Hub, which compiles them into tidy .tflite binaries. The interesting part was the errors. One warned that float16 "isn't supported" — except it is, as long as the model boundary stays float32 and the runtime downcasts later. Another complained about the NPU, which turned out to be a normal execution path, not a failure. Most "crashes" I chased were actually just Java memory habits on the Android side. Once I could tell the difference between a real error and compiler theater, the system stabilized.

Why this matters

A self-driving stack that fits in a phone isn't a party trick. It's a signal that autonomous perception doesn't have to be locked behind expensive hardware — that a student with a dev phone and patience can build the same pipeline the big labs do, just smaller. That's the part I find worth writing down.
