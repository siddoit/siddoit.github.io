We Built a Robot That Yells at You for Slouching

Published: May 20, 2026 · IoT

Some projects teach you something. This one mostly taught a machine to nag. It started as a tethered sensor board and turned into a wireless edge node that watches how you sit, reads your mood, and physically buzzes when you've had enough.

The edge node that stopped freezing

The ESP32 became a little data hub: a DHT11 for temperature and humidity, a BH1750 light sensor over I2C, and — the fun part — an I2S mic, not a cheap analog one. We wrote the math to turn raw 32-bit audio into an RMS decibel reading right on the chip. The light sensor liked to crash and take the whole board down with it, so we added a software fail-safe that resets the I2C bus instead of dying. Then we threw out the serial cables and moved everything to Wi-Fi UDP, so the board could blast data to the laptop and listen for buzzer commands at the same time without blocking.

The brains

Vision ran on a trimmed OpenCV feed (DirectShow, single-frame buffer, no lag) with MediaPipe pulling face boxes and 3D skeleton landmarks. On top of that sat two small models: a custom posture network that takes 17 landmarks, works out joint angles, and says "good / slouching / leaning," and an EfficientNet emotion CNN reading micro-expressions into anger, fear, sadness, disgust.

The part that makes it smart

Raw signals are noise, so we built a scoring engine: 35% posture, 35% emotion, 30% environment. And to stop one glance at your phone from setting off alarms, a rolling buffer of the last 50 frames smooths everything out. Sustained stress maps to actual advice — thoracic extensions for slouching, box breathing for the frazzled.

When it decides you've had enough

Critical stress triggers a three-way strike, all off the main video loop via threads: a Telegram ping to your phone, a hard Windows pop-up it can't ignore, and a UDP payload back to the ESP32 that fires the physical buzzer in the room. The backend later moved into a Flask app, and we pulled every API key out of the source into a .env so it could go public without leaking anything.

Why it's a fun one

It's the rare project where the interesting bit isn't the model — it's the plumbing. A sensor that resets itself, a score that ignores flinches, and a buzzer that means business. Build that and you've got something that feels less like a demo and more like a colleague with opinions.
